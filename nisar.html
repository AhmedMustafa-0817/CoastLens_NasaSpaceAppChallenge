NISAR · NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar

A torch in orbit.

NISAR is a satellite. It does not take normal photos. It sends radio waves down to Earth, like a torch you cannot see, and it listens for the waves that come back. This page explains it in short, simple steps.

01

A camera needs light. Radar makes its own.

A normal satellite camera needs sunlight and a clear sky. NISAR sends its own radio waves down to Earth. So it can see at night, and it can see through clouds.

02

Water looks dark. Forest looks bright.

Radio waves hit flat water and bounce away, so the water looks dark. They hit trees and branches and bounce back to the satellite, so the forest looks bright. More bounce means a brighter picture.

Bars show our measured NISAR echo (HV) over Karachi's coast, 23 Oct 2026: how much of the wave came back. Closer to zero means brighter. Town and other land includes buildings, which echo strongly too.

05

It comes back every 12 days.

One picture shows one moment. Pictures every 12 days show change over time. If the forest gets thinner, the echo gets weaker. If nothing changes, the two pictures match.

06

Radar is honest. It cannot read minds.

What it can tell us
  • Where there is forest, and where there is open water.
  • Whether the echo got stronger or weaker between two dates.
  • Whether the place stayed the same over 12 days.
What it cannot tell us alone
  • Why something changed. Tide, rain and wet mud also change the echo.
  • Whether a tree is healthy, or how many trees there are.
  • Anything about fish, money or people. That needs people on the ground.

Words to know
Satellite
A machine that flies around the Earth and looks down at it.
Radar
A way to see using radio waves instead of light.
Radio waves
Invisible waves. Our phones use them too.
Echo
The part of the wave that bounces back to the satellite.
L-band
Long radio waves. They reach into forests and trunks.
Mangrove
Trees that grow in salty water, with roots in the mud.
