NISAR · NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar

A torch in orbit.

NISAR is a satellite that does not take photographs. It shines its own invisible light at the Earth and listens for what comes back. Six short steps, no jargon.

30 Jul 2025launched from Sriharikota, India
747 kmabove the Earth
12 mwide radar antenna, like a giant umbrella
12 daysuntil it passes the same place again
01

A camera waits for sunlight. Radar brings its own.

A normal satellite is a camera. It needs daylight and a clear sky. NISAR sends out a pulse of radio waves, like a torch beam you cannot see, so night and cloud do not matter.

02

It measures how much comes back.

Flat water acts like a mirror and sends the pulse away, so it looks dark. A forest is a tangle of trunks and branches that scatters the pulse back, so it looks bright. That difference is the whole trick.

Bars: our measured NISAR echo (HV) over Karachi's coast, 23 Oct 2026. Closer to zero is brighter. Town and other land includes buildings, which echo strongly too.

05

Then it comes back. Every 12 days.

One picture is a snapshot. A picture every 12 days is a heartbeat. If the forest thins, the echo fades. If nothing changes, two passes match.

06

Radar is honest. It is not a mind reader.

What it can tell us
  • Where there is standing forest, and where there is open water.
  • Whether the echo from a place got stronger or weaker between passes.
  • Whether the surface stayed the same over 12 days.
What it cannot tell us alone
  • Why something changed. Tide, rain and wet mud change the echo too.
  • Whether a tree is healthy, or how many trees there are.
  • Anything about fish, income or people. That needs people on the ground.
